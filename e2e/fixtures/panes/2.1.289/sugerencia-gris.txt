  molecules, releasing oxygen gas as a byproduct and freeing hydrogen ions into
  the thylakoid interior. The excited electrons travel down an electron
  transport chain, and the energy they give up pumps hydrogen ions across the
  membrane, building a concentration gradient. As the ions flow back out through
  an enzyme called ATP synthase, they drive the production of ATP, the cell's
  main energy currency. At the end of the chain, the electrons are used to
  convert NADP⁺ into NADPH, a molecule that carries high-energy electrons for
  later use. ATP and NADPH then power the Calvin cycle, which runs in the
  stroma, the fluid surrounding the thylakoids, and doesn't need light directly.
  In this cycle, the enzyme rubisco attaches carbon dioxide from the air to a
  five-carbon sugar called RuBP, and the resulting compounds are reduced using
  ATP and NADPH to form three-carbon sugars. Some of these sugars leave the
  cycle to be built into glucose, starch, and cellulose, while the rest are used
  to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can continue. Overall, the process turns
  carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, which supplies the food and
  the oxygen that nearly all life on Earth depends on.
